# Topic opportunity research

Use this guide for a deep topic scan. Re-check current official YouTube guidance and the live Studio interface when needed; feature names, availability, and reporting can change.

## Evidence sources

Use sources in roughly this order, while recording what was actually accessible:

1. **Creator's own YouTube Studio evidence, if supplied or already accessible:** Trends insights; Audience reports such as what viewers watch and channels they watch; video Reach traffic sources and YouTube search terms; and available impressions, click-through, and watch-behavior reports. Also use dated `analytics/` performance reviews when present, honoring their scope and confidence. Compare metrics only across compatible formats, surfaces, time windows, and samples. These can ground the channel-specific opportunity more strongly than public estimates. Reports may be limited, sampled, or unavailable for some channels and queries. Never request login credentials.
2. **The channel's public catalogue:** recent uploads, relevant older videos, format and topic patterns, titles/thumbnails, visible views and dates, and a small sample of comments. State the sample and date. Public performance is not a substitute for impressions, retention, search terms, audience mix, or satisfaction data.
3. **YouTube topic evidence:** search the exact query and natural variants in the target market and language; inspect a manageable set of current results for intent, recency, format, audience promise, and what existing videos do or fail to do. Check autocomplete or related searches as qualitative clues only. Results can vary by location, account, and time; note the query, market, and check date.
4. **Trend and external context:** use Google Trends with the relevant geography, timeframe, and YouTube Search property when available; use official product, release, pricing, event, or platform sources to verify timely claims. Google Trends is normalized relative interest, not absolute search volume. A missing or low-volume chart is not proof that nobody cares.
5. **Comparable creators:** identify a few channels serving a similar viewer or job, not just the largest creators. Use their videos to understand current coverage, framing, and format—not as proof of demand or permission to copy their ideas.

YouTube's Studio Trends tab can expose audience-related top searches, breakout videos, recent videos, and (for Shorts) content gaps, but some insights depend on country, language, device, and data availability. The Studio Audience and Reach reports can also show what viewers watch and the searches that brought viewers to a video. Ask for a screenshot or export only when private evidence materially improves the decision and cannot be accessed otherwise; the public-only analysis should still proceed.

For a practical public channel snapshot, begin with roughly the latest 10 relevant uploads (or the active period if fewer), then inspect a few relevant back-catalogue examples. Note the sample window and separate long-form, Shorts, and live content. Use older high-view videos as clues to topics and formats, not as a forecast; visible lifetime views favor older uploads. Expand the sample only when it could change the recommendation.

## Research the discovery path

Classify each opportunity by its strongest plausible route; use more than one only when justified. Search in the audience's approved language, script, and natural code-switching patterns. For Hinglish audiences, check relevant Hindi/Devanagari, Roman Hindi, and English product or model terms where viewers plausibly use them; do not assume every query should be translated literally.

- **Search-led:** a clear query or recurring viewer problem, identifiable intent, and a video that can answer it better or more usefully than current results.
- **Recommendation-led:** a compelling viewer problem, product, comparison, story, or surprising demonstration that can earn a clear title/thumbnail promise beyond people already searching for the exact phrase.
- **Timely:** a verified release, price change, update, event, or trend with a real publishing window. Record the date and why the timing matters.
- **Evergreen or series:** a durable need or a repeatable format supported by channel fit and evidence from existing videos or audience behavior.

Do not make every idea target a keyword. YouTube describes Search around relevance, engagement, and quality, while recommendations are personalized around what viewers watch and enjoy. For each idea, ask whether the channel can make a useful, credible video and package it accurately—not just whether a phrase exists.

## Evaluate the opportunity

Compare candidates using evidence-backed judgments, not fabricated precision:

- **Audience and channel fit:** Does this serve a known or plausible viewer need and fit approved channel direction? Is it a useful adjacent expansion or an unexplained departure?
- **Demand signal:** What concrete evidence suggests people want it now or repeatedly? Separate Studio evidence, YouTube result patterns, Trends interest, and anecdotal comments. State limitations.
- **Opportunity gap:** What is missing, hard to find, dated, confusing, poorly demonstrated, or insufficiently localized in existing coverage? Name the evidence; do not assume that low competition means high demand.
- **Creator advantage:** What access, experience, expertise, testing, assets, or presentation style can this creator bring? For hands-on reviews, do not assume product experience; use Review Grill if firsthand experience must be collected.
- **Feasibility and timing:** Is there time to research, test, film, and publish while the opportunity is useful? Identify required proof, budget, access, and likely effort.
- **Shelf life and portfolio value:** Is this one-off, seasonal, evergreen, or a possible series? Does it broaden the audience responsibly or deepen an existing promise?

Rank the shortlist by the creator's requested goal. If no goal is stated, balance viewer value, channel fit, evidence strength, distinctiveness, and feasibility. Keep **opportunity priority** separate from **evidence confidence**: a high-upside idea can still have low confidence. Explain any close calls instead of hiding them behind a composite score.

## Idea brief format

For each shortlisted idea, include:

- **Working concept** and one-sentence viewer promise.
- **Target viewer and need**, including the question, task, or decision the video helps with.
- **Discovery route:** Search, recommendation, timely, evergreen/series, or a justified combination. Include natural query variants only when observed or clearly labeled as hypotheses.
- **Channel-specific angle:** why this fits the current channel and what the creator can uniquely show or explain.
- **Evidence:** links, checked dates, geography/language, and what each source supports. Include a few representative competing videos when useful and identify the gap without copying their framing.
- **Required proof and effort:** demonstrations, product access, tests, expertise, or production work needed; mark anything unconfirmed.
- **Priority and confidence:** high/medium/low with a short reason for each. Do not call this a forecast.
- **What to learn after publishing:** one useful follow-up signal to check in Studio, matched to the idea's goal (for example, Search traffic for a query-led answer, or impressions, click-through, and watch behavior for a recommendation-led package). Suggest review rather than an unsupported pass/fail threshold.
- **Packaging seed:** one accurate title direction and thumbnail idea that communicate the same promise. These are hypotheses for later development, not the finished Video Kit upload package.
- **Risks or expiry:** stale prices, release timing, unsupported claims, policy issues, crowded coverage, or missing creator experience.

End with the best next idea to make and why, plus the main assumption that could change that recommendation. If evidence is thin, say so and propose the smallest validation step (for example, check Studio Trends, inspect a search query, test a community poll, or confirm product access). Do not turn every shortlist into a questionnaire.

## Keep the research current

Date-stamp time-sensitive evidence. Recheck product versions, local pricing/availability, and release information near production. Treat search results, trend charts, public views, and comments as noisy signals; triangulate before ranking. Record sources so Video Kit can reuse relevant research and verify volatile facts before scripting.
